## The Question

This one did not start with a technology. It started with my wife wanting something that
did not seem to exist: a way to see what was happening in different parts of the world at
the same moment, and how the people and events involved were connected to each other.

Encyclopaedias store history as documents. But history is not a set of documents. It is a
set of relationships between people, places and events that shift over time.

I wondered what happens if you model the relationships first and let the pages fall out of
the graph.

## The Problem

If you want to know about the Norman conquest, the internet serves you well. If you want to
know what was happening in West Africa, China and Mesoamerica during the same decades, and
whether any of it was connected, you are on your own. You end up with eight browser tabs,
three timelines that disagree, and a mental model you have to rebuild every time you come
back to it.

The gap is not a lack of information. It is that the information is stored as articles. An
article is a good container for a narrative and a poor container for a question like "who
else was alive when this person was, and did their lives intersect?" Answering that means
reading dozens of documents and holding the dates in your head, because nothing in the
storage format knows that two dates overlap.

Conventional timelines do not fix it either. A timeline is a single axis, usually a single
region, and it flattens the thing I actually wanted: the connections that run sideways
across places and forwards through time.

## The Hypothesis

My hypothesis was that time and relationships should be first-class data rather than prose
that happens to mention dates.

If an event knows when it happened, where it happened, who was involved, and which other
events it caused or followed, then "what else was happening then?" stops being a research
task and becomes a query. The narrative can be generated from the structure, rather than
the structure being inferred from the narrative.

That was the bet. It is worth saying plainly that it was a bet about modelling, not a claim
about accuracy — a graph is only as trustworthy as the sources behind it, and I
underestimated how much of the work that sentence hides.

## The Approach

I started by trying to model a single century in one region by hand. Not a system, just
entities and relationships written down, to find out whether the model I had in my head
survived contact with real historical detail.

It did not, which was useful. Working through a small set of real events forced the
questions I had been avoiding: what counts as an event as opposed to a period, whether a
person and a role are the same thing, and what to do with a date that is a guess.

From there the work went in three passes:

1. **Model the shape.** Entities for people, places, events, periods and sources, with
   typed relationships between them and time attached to the relationship rather than only
   to the entity.
2. **Load real data.** Import enough material to break the model, then fix the model rather
   than the data.
3. **Make it explorable.** Build a mobile application on top, because the interaction I
   wanted — follow a thread, then follow it sideways — is a good fit for a phone and a bad
   fit for a page of search results.

## Architecture

The core is a temporal knowledge graph in Neo4j. Nodes represent people, places, events,
periods and sources. Edges represent participation, causation, succession, location and
attribution, and it is the edges that carry the time information, because the interesting
question is usually when a relationship held rather than when a thing existed.

A .NET service sits in front of the graph and owns ingestion, entity resolution and the
read API. PostgreSQL holds the things a graph is bad at: import batches, source metadata,
provenance records and the flat lookups that back search. The mobile application talks only
to the .NET API, never to the graph directly, so the traversal patterns stay something I can
change.

Three decisions did most of the work:

1. **Time lives on relationships.** A person node does not carry a single date range. "Was
   ruler of" carries the range, "was present at" carries the moment. This made overlap
   queries natural and made a lot of my earlier modelling look naive.
2. **Uncertainty is part of the model, not a caveat in the text.** Every temporal value has
   a precision and a confidence alongside it, so "1066" and "some time in the eleventh
   century" are different values rather than the same value with a footnote.
3. **Every claim points at a source.** Facts are attached to the source that asserts them.
   Two sources may assert contradictory things, and the graph stores both.

## Trade-offs

- **Graph versus relational.** A relational schema would have been faster to build and
  easier to host. It would also have made every question I actually cared about a
  multi-join exercise. I took the operational cost of a graph database to keep the
  traversals cheap, and I still hold both stores, which is complexity I own.
- **Modelling fidelity versus getting anything finished.** Every historical edge case
  suggests another distinction worth making. I repeatedly had to decide that a distinction
  was real, interesting, and out of scope.
- **Generated narrative versus authored narrative.** Text assembled from the graph is
  consistent and slightly lifeless. Authored text reads better and drifts out of sync with
  the data. I have not resolved this; the current answer is that the graph is the source of
  truth and prose is a view over it, which I am not fully happy with.
- **Depth versus scope.** Modelling one region well and one century deeply produces
  something demonstrably useful. Modelling everything shallowly produces something that
  looks impressive and answers nothing. I chose depth, which means large parts of history
  are simply absent.

## What Went Wrong

My first model treated dates as values. Every event had a start and an end, both timestamps,
and I moved on to the interesting parts feeling quite pleased.

Historical dates are not timestamps. They are claims of varying precision, sometimes
mutually exclusive, frequently expressed relative to something else, and occasionally
recorded in a calendar that no longer applies. My schema could store "1066" perfectly and
could not store "probably in the spring, according to one chronicle written a century later"
at all. So I did what the schema encouraged: I picked whichever date looked most
authoritative and discarded the rest.

That quietly destroyed the thing the project existed for. Once you have thrown away the
disagreement between sources, you cannot show a user that historians are unsure, and you
cannot explain why two parts of the graph contradict each other. Worse, the confident single
date makes the system look more reliable than it is, which is the failure mode I would least
want in something about history.

The fix meant reworking the temporal model into precision and confidence carried on the
relationship, and reworking ingestion so that conflicting claims are both retained and
attributed. It was the largest rewrite in the project and it came from a modelling decision
I made in an afternoon without thinking hard enough.

Entity resolution went wrong in a smaller, more embarrassing way. Matching people across
sources by name and approximate date collapses distinct people with common names into one
node and splits one person recorded under variant spellings into several. I now treat
resolution as a suggestion that needs review rather than something to run automatically.

## Lessons

- The hard part of a knowledge graph is not the graph. It is deciding what the nodes are,
  and that decision is a domain question rather than a technical one.
- Uncertainty has to be a type in the model. If you represent doubt as prose, the first
  query that touches the data will discard it.
- Attaching claims to sources is not academic tidiness. It is what makes contradiction
  representable instead of a bug.
- Building a small part of the domain properly taught me far more than planning the whole of
  it. The hand-modelled century is still the best thing I did on this project.
- A system that presents a confident answer where the evidence is thin is not merely
  incomplete, it is misleading. That applies well beyond history.

## Current State

Chronos is growing rather than finished. The graph model and the .NET service work,
ingestion runs, and the mobile application supports the exploration pattern the project was
built for. It is a personal prototype: I have not published it, I have not measured it
against anyone else's needs beyond the original request, and the coverage of history in it
is narrow by design.

The repository is private, largely because source licensing and attribution for the imported
material still needs a proper review before any of it goes public. I am happy to walk
through the model, the API and the parts of the ingestion pipeline that are mine.

## Next Questions

- What is the clearest single example of a connection an ordinary timeline hides? I still do
  not have the one demonstration that makes the whole idea obvious in ten seconds.
- How should uncertainty be shown in the interface? The model represents it well and the
  application still renders it as a caveat, which is the old mistake in a new place.
- Where should the boundary sit between the authored graph and generated assistance? A
  language model can produce fluent history, and fluent history is exactly what this project
  was meant to be sceptical of.
- Which parts of the mobile application are stable enough to show, and what would I need to
  check about source licensing before showing them?
