## The Question

Morris did not begin as an AI project. It began as a physics simulation: a field of coupled
oscillators, each with a phase, nudging its neighbours, damped so the whole thing settles
instead of exploding. The second commit in the repository is called "is now working with the
right math", which tells you what I was worried about at the time.

What kept me looking at it was that the field settles into stable regions. Patches of the
simulation lock into agreement with each other and stay there. And a stable, repeatable,
addressable region of state is — if you squint, and I did — a register.

So the question stopped being about waves. I wondered whether a simulation could be a
computer, and whether that computer could be made to reason in a way I could watch.

  Morris is an experiment. It is not a product, not a step towards machine minds, and not a system I

  than from memory, and rewriting it corrected several things I had believed about my own project.

</Callout>

## The Problem

The problem I actually had is the one that sits under most of AI, and I arrived at it
sideways rather than by reading about it.

Continuous systems — wave fields, neural networks, anything made of floating point — are
good at settling, generalising and degrading gracefully. They are bad at telling you what
they mean. There is no point in the tensor where you can put your finger and say "that is
the idea of a door".

Discrete symbolic systems are the exact inverse. A rule that fires is a rule you can read,
and when the conclusion is wrong you can name the rule responsible. But somebody has to
write every rule, the rules do not stretch past what was anticipated, and the encoding cost
of a messy domain grows faster than the value you get back.

The usual move is to pick one and apologise for the other. What I wanted was a continuous
substrate that was nevertheless completely inspectable — because it is my simulation, and
every number in it is available — and then a way to read discrete symbols out of it and
write them back in.

## The Hypothesis

My hypothesis was that the boundary between continuous and discrete could be a round trip
rather than a wall.

Concretely: if I threshold the state of the field, I get discrete symbols. If those symbols
drive rules, I get inference I can read. And if the conclusions of that inference can be
written back into the field as a perturbation — a phase shift applied to a region — then
reasoning and simulation are not two systems bolted together. They are one loop, and I can
watch every step of it.

That was the bet. It is worth saying immediately that the bet has a flaw I did not see
clearly until much later, and the flaw is in the word "threshold". More on that below.

<Callout variant="note" title="What is actually in the repository">
  **Built and running:** a PyTorch simulation of a coupled phase field; a simulated CPU whose
  registers are regions of that field, with its own instruction set; a knowledge base that
  extracts symbols from field state, forward-chains rules over them and seeds symbols back into
  the field; RippleLang, a small imperative macro language, with a parser, a virtual machine and
  an execution trace; translation from natural language into RippleLang by a locally hosted
  language model; and Morris itself — a conversational front end with persistent memory, tone
  control and a Windows voice client.

**Built and honestly not load-bearing:** roughly seventeen separate services behind Docker,
PostgreSQL, Redis and a message queue, for a system with exactly one user.

**Never built, despite what I used to say about it:** any vision or perception pipeline. There
is none. An earlier version of this page claimed 3D vision fed structured facts into the
symbolic layer. That was aspiration that had quietly turned into recollection.

</Callout>

## The Approach

The order things were built in is the honest explanation of the project, because each layer
exists to make the previous one useful.

The field came first. Phase and amplitude across a grid of oscillators, coupled to their
neighbours, integrated in small time steps with a damping term. Written in PyTorch, mostly
because I wanted the derivatives and the GPU, and partly because it made the whole state a
tensor I could print.

The CPU came next, and this is the part I still find pleasing. Registers are not variables;
they are zones of the phase field. Reading a register means taking a statistic over a region
of the simulation. Writing to one means perturbing that region and letting the physics carry
it. There is an instruction memory, an arithmetic unit and an instruction pointer, and the
whole thing steps forward alongside the physics rather than on top of it.

Then the symbolic layer, which is the round trip from the hypothesis. It watches the field
and emits symbols when conditions hold — the phase in this region is high, the variance
across this task exceeds this value, overall coherence is above this line. Those symbols feed
a rule set, rules fire when all their antecedents are present, and conclusions can be seeded
back into the field as a phase shift.

RippleLang came after that, because driving the system by hand had become tedious. It is a
small imperative macro language: seed a symbol, wait, branch if a value exceeds a threshold,
jump to a label, call an action or a plugin, return. Programs are parsed to instructions and
run on a virtual machine that keeps a trace of everything it executed.

And then the language model, which does one job: turn a sentence into a RippleLang macro. It
runs locally rather than against a hosted service. Morris — the name, the voice, the persona
— is the layer in front of that, and it arrived last.

## Architecture

Five layers, and the interesting property is that the bottom two are the same substrate.

1. **The field.** A simulated grid of coupled oscillators carrying phase and amplitude,
   integrated in small steps with coupling and damping constants I tuned by watching it. This
   is the only part of the system with no abstraction underneath it.
2. **The CPU.** Registers mapped to regions of the field, an instruction memory, an
   arithmetic unit and a program counter. A register value is a statistic over a patch of
   simulation, so computation and physics are the same substrate rather than layered.
3. **The symbolic layer.** Symbol extraction by thresholding field statistics, a forward-
   chaining rule engine over the resulting symbol set, and a seeding path that writes
   conclusions back into the field as perturbations. It also keeps transition counts between
   symbols over time, which is where the surprise detection and counterfactual work sits.
4. **RippleLang and its machine.** Parser, instruction set and interpreter, with an execution
   trace recorded per run. Exposed over an HTTP service with validate, execute and step-
   through-debug endpoints.
5. **Model and persona.** A locally hosted language model behind a gateway, used to translate
   natural language into RippleLang and to rewrite macros. Morris sits in front: tone modes,
   safety filters, a memory store and a WebSocket interface, plus a Windows client with a
   wake word and speech synthesis.

Around all of that is a Docker composition of roughly seventeen services with PostgreSQL,
Redis and a message queue. That is the part of the architecture I would not repeat.

## Trade-offs

- **A physics substrate versus just writing the program.** Nothing Morris computes needs a
  wave simulation underneath it. I could have written the rule engine on its own in an
  afternoon and it would have been faster, clearer and less interesting. I chose the
  substrate because the question was whether it could be done, not whether it should be, and
  I would make the same choice again for the same reason — while being honest that it is not
  an engineering justification.
- **Continuous state versus discrete symbols.** The round trip works. What it costs is that
  the translation between the two is lossy in both directions and I control the loss by hand.
  That is the central trade and I do not think I got it right.
- **An imperative language versus the declarative one I wanted.** RippleLang has labels and
  jumps because that is what the machine underneath it wanted. I had intended something
  closer to facts, rules and goals, and what I built is closer to assembly with a friendly
  face. The rule system ended up living in the host language instead, which means the thing
  I can most easily read is not the thing I most wanted to read.
- **Seventeen services for one user.** Splitting the system up made each piece testable and
  made the whole thing tedious to run. There is a superseded monolithic orchestrator archived
  in the repository that was, for actually doing research, better.
- **A local model versus a hosted one.** Running the model locally kept everything on one
  machine and kept costs at zero. It also meant model quality was whatever fitted in memory,
  and a meaningful share of the bad translations were the small model's fault rather than
  the architecture's — which makes the architecture harder to evaluate, not easier.

## What Went Wrong

The failure I want to describe is not dramatic, and it is the one that matters.

**The thresholds were mine.** Symbols come out of the field when a statistic crosses a
line — phase above this, variance beyond that, coherence over this other number. I chose
every one of those lines by hand, by watching the simulation and picking values that produced
a sensible-looking number of symbols. Which means the vocabulary the "symbolic" layer reasons
in is not something the system discovered in the physics. It is something I installed and
then observed with delight. Every interesting behaviour I saw has to survive that objection
first, and I cannot currently show that any of them do. This is the flaw in the hypothesis,
it was there from the first version, and it took me far too long to state plainly.

**Things degraded silently into mocks.** Several integration points fall back to stub
implementations when an import fails, log a debug line, and carry on. It is a reasonable
pattern for a service that must stay up. It is a terrible one for a research system, because
a run that appears to work may not have been running the components you were testing. For a
project whose entire premise is inspectable state, having a code path that quietly replaces
the thing being inspected is close to self-parody.

**The system could rewrite its own program, and I thought that was a feature.** The
instruction set includes an operation that writes a new instruction into the instruction
memory, so a running program can modify itself mid-execution. Separately, there was a module
that pruned rules whose success rate fell below a cutoff, deleting parts of the rule set
between runs. Both were deliberate. Both did what they were supposed to. And together they
meant that when behaviour changed I could not always tell whether I had changed it, the
program had, or the rule pruner had removed something three runs ago that everything since
had depended on. The rule-pruning module is orphaned in the current tree; the self-modifying
instruction survives, in the simulation, where it can only rewrite a tensor.

**Containment was thinner than I told myself.** There is an action plugin that launches
applications on the host, including a shell, through the operating system. There is a
plugin that reads and writes files, and there are plugins that make outbound network calls.
Plugins are discovered by scanning a directory at start-up and importing whatever Python
files are there, so the capability list is not a fixed thing declared in one place — it is
whatever is on disk. The desktop client keeps an allow-list of permitted commands, which is
real and does work; the sandbox flag next to it is off by default. None of that is exotic and
none of it was hidden from me. It was simply less contained than the version of the project
I carried around in my head, and the state label at the top of this page is a joke that was
doing more work than it had earned.

The honest summary of the last two is that I had been describing intentions as properties.
Reading the repository to write this page is what corrected it.

## Lessons

- If you choose the vocabulary a system reasons in, you cannot then be impressed by what it
  reasons about. This is the whole lesson of the project and everything else is detail.
- A safety property that is not enforced in one readable place is not a property. It is a
  habit, and habits do not survive the next plugin you drop in a folder.
- Silent fallbacks and inspectable state are incompatible goals. Research code should fail
  loudly and let me deal with it.
- Writing something down for other people to read is a stronger audit than any review I
  perform on my own work. Three claims on this page did not survive contact with the
  repository, and I believed all three sincerely.
- Building the beautiful substrate first and the useful thing on top means the useful thing
  is shaped by the substrate. Registers made of wave regions gave me an assembly language,
  not the declarative language I actually wanted.
- Ten weeks is enough time to build something genuinely novel and nowhere near enough time
  to find out whether it is any good.

## Current State

Morris is stopped. The repository holds 177 commits between late May and mid-July 2025, and
nothing since. It runs, or it did the last time I brought the composition up: the field
simulates, the CPU executes, the rule engine fires, RippleLang parses and runs, and the local
model turns sentences into macros with a hit rate somewhere around encouraging.

There is a reasonable test suite — around a hundred test files covering the language runtime,
the reasoning components and the services. There is no evaluation of any kind against a
baseline. I never established that the physics substrate does anything a plain rule engine
would not, which is the question the whole project rests on, and it remains unanswered.

The repository is private. Partly that is tidiness, and partly it is that the write-up above
is a more honest account of the system than the repository's own documentation, which
contains benchmark figures and completion claims I cannot reproduce.

## Next Questions

- Can symbols be derived from the field rather than thresholded out of it by hand? Unless the
  answer is yes, the interesting version of this project has not been attempted.
- What is the smallest baseline that would settle whether the substrate earns its place — the
  same rule set, the same tasks, no physics underneath?
- Would the declarative language I originally wanted survive contact with a machine whose
  registers are regions of a wave field, or is the imperative shape forced?
- How much of what I attributed to the architecture was the small local model, and how would
  I separate the two?
- What would a capability list look like if it could not be extended by adding a file to a
  folder? That is the fix, and I have not written it.
